\section{Creep Behavior in Metallic Materials}
\label{Section::Creep_Behavior_in_Metallic_Materials}

\subsection{Fundamentals of Creep}
\label{Section::Fundamentals_of_Creep}

Creep is a time-dependent, permanent deformation that occurs in materials subjected to constant stress and elevated temperatures, typically above 30\% of their melting temperature in Kelvin \cite{frost1982deformation}. In metals, creep can significantly influence the mechanical integrity of components operating in high-temperature environments such as gas turbines, nuclear reactors, and jet engines.

The creep strain, $\epsilon(t)$, in a material is a function of both time and temperature and can be expressed empirically through various models. The rate at which creep deformation occurs is influenced by several factors, including applied stress, grain size, temperature, and the presence of second-phase particles or precipitates \cite{frost1982deformation, dieter1986mechanical}. 


\subsection{Stages of Creep: Primary, Secondary, and Tertiary}

A typical creep curve, plotting strain versus time under constant load and temperature, consists of three distinct stages:

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figures/creep_stages.png}
	\caption{Schematic of the three stages of creep behavior in metals.}
	\label{fig:creep_stages}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Primary Creep:}  
	Also known as transient creep, this stage is characterized by a decreasing creep rate. It results from the competition between strain hardening and recovery mechanisms. Dislocations multiply and interact, increasing internal resistance to further deformation.
	
	\item \textbf{Secondary Creep:}  
	Also referred to as steady-state creep, this stage features a nearly constant creep rate. It reflects a dynamic balance between work hardening and recovery. This stage is the most predictable and often used for long-term design analysis.
	
	\item \textbf{Tertiary Creep:}  
	In this final stage, the creep rate accelerates until failure. Mechanisms such as grain boundary void formation, microcracking, and necking dominate, leading to localized damage and rupture. This stage is typically excluded from design due to its unpredictability.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Mechanisms of Creep Deformation}

Creep deformation in metals is governed by several atomic and dislocation-level mechanisms. The dominant mechanism depends on temperature, stress, grain size, and diffusion conditions.

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/creep_mechanisms.png}
	\caption{Overview of different creep mechanisms based on stress and temperature \cite{wessel2004modelling}.}
	\label{fig:creep_mechanisms}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Dislocation (Power-Law) Creep:}  
	Dominant at intermediate temperatures and high stresses. It involves the motion of dislocations through the crystal lattice, and the creep rate follows a power-law relationship with stress. Creep strain rate is given by:
	\begin{equation}
		\dot{\varepsilon} = A \sigma^n \exp\left(-\frac{Q}{RT}\right)
		\label{eq:powerlaw}
	\end{equation}
	where $n$ is the stress exponent (typically 3–8), $Q$ is activation energy, $R$ is the gas constant, and $T$ is absolute temperature.
	
	\item \textbf{Nabarro-Herring Creep:}  
	Occurs at high temperatures and low stresses in materials with large grain sizes. Atoms diffuse through the lattice, causing elongation in the direction of the applied stress and contraction perpendicular to it.
	
	\item \textbf{Coble Creep:}  
	Similar to Nabarro-Herring creep but involves diffusion along grain boundaries instead of through the bulk. It is significant in fine-grained materials and becomes prominent at lower temperatures than Nabarro-Herring creep.
	
	\item \textbf{Grain Boundary Sliding (GBS):}  
	Grain boundaries slide past each other, sometimes accommodated by dislocation motion or diffusion. This mechanism becomes more significant at very high temperatures or under cyclic thermal loading.
	
	\item \textbf{Climb-Glide Mechanism:}  
	Dislocations move by glide in specific planes and climb to overcome obstacles by absorbing or emitting vacancies. This enables continued plastic flow even when glide is hindered.
	
\end{itemize}

These mechanisms may act simultaneously, with the dominant mechanism shifting depending on the material and operating environment. Detailed studies, such as those by Chatterjee and Roy \cite{chatterjee_creep}, have shown that microstructural features like precipitate coarsening, dislocation density evolution, and subgrain formation play critical roles in determining creep performance.

\subsection{Phenomenological and Semi-Physical Creep Models}

Creep has been modeled using both empirical and semi-physical approaches:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Norton’s Law:} Equation~$\ref{eq:powerlaw}$ models steady-state creep with a power-law stress dependency.
	\item \textbf{Larson–Miller Parameter (LMP):} Converts long-term creep tests into master curves using a time–temperature parameter \cite{larson1954time}.
	\item \textbf{Monkman–Grant Relationship:} Links minimum creep rate with rupture time empirically \cite{monkman1956relationship}.
	\item \textbf{Wilshire Models:} Incorporate yield stress into the stress exponent for a more physical interpretation \cite{wilshire2007creep}.
\end{itemize}

Recent studies also emphasize the comparative effectiveness of creep extrapolation models. For instance, the work of Sattar et al. evaluates curve fitting of the Kachanov–Rabotnov model to Norton–Bailey law using regression-based damage evolution for SS-316 material \cite{sattar2021damage}, providing a structured path for analytical creep life extension.


\subsection{Microstructural and Physical Models}

Dislocation-based and damage-informed models provide more accuracy for long-term predictions:

\begin{equation}
	\dot{\varepsilon} = \frac{\rho_m b v_g}{M}
\end{equation}

where $\rho_m$ is mobile dislocation density, $b$ the Burgers vector, and $v_g$ the dislocation velocity. Work from Ghoniem and the ULiege research group extends this by evolving internal variables like:

\begin{itemize}
	\item Static and mobile dislocation densities
	\item Subgrain radius and boundary effects
	\item Cavitation and damage
\end{itemize}

Such models are implemented in FEM tools like \texttt{Lagamine} to simulate thermal fatigue and multi-axial creep \cite{morch2022creep}.


These models are limited in their ability to generalize across materials and loading conditions, prompting the need for simulation- and ML-based approaches.


\section{Numerical Modeling of Creep}

\subsection{Finite Element Approaches}

Finite element analysis (FEA) provides a powerful framework for simulating creep deformation in complex geometries and loading conditions. Constitutive models describing creep behavior are embedded into material subroutines and solved within FE codes. Lagamine, the FEA tool used in this thesis, includes time-integration schemes suitable for large strain, time-dependent plasticity problems, enabling robust modeling of the full creep lifecycle.

Numerical models offer the advantage of extrapolating creep behavior under untested conditions. However, these models require extensive calibration and may become computationally expensive for high-dimensional parametric studies.

\subsection{Creep Law Implementations}

Several phenomenological creep laws have been implemented in finite element solvers, such as:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Norton-Bailey Law:} Useful for steady-state creep, with creep strain rate expressed as a power-law function of stress and temperature.
	\item \textbf{$\theta$-Projection Models:} Introduced to represent all three creep stages with fitted parameters \cite{brown2002extrapolation}.
	\item \textbf{Time Hardening and Strain Hardening Laws:} Capture primary and secondary creep stages by modifying the effective stress as a function of accumulated creep strain or time.
\end{itemize}

Such models are often calibrated against experimental or synthetic data using optimization techniques. The implementation of these models in software like Lagamine enables generation of creep curves for a wide range of loading and material parameters. 

The modified theta projection method has also been proposed for capturing all creep stages using fewer empirical parameters, enhancing its extrapolation capabilities \cite{liu2020theta}. This aligns well with the trends in creep law implementations in FE codes.


\subsection{Limitations of Classical Numerical Methods}

Despite their accuracy, classical numerical models face several limitations:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{High computational cost:} Especially when exploring large material parameter spaces or performing uncertainty quantification.
	\item \textbf{Sensitivity to parameterization:} Requires precise identification of model constants, which may not be available from experiments.
	\item \textbf{Limited extrapolation:} Classical models may fail outside calibrated regimes or when microstructural effects are dominant.
\end{itemize}

As described in \cite{wessel2004modelling}, these challenges motivate the development of surrogate models that approximate the numerical outputs with significantly reduced computational effort.


\section{Machine Learning in Materials Science}

In recent years, machine learning (ML) has emerged as a transformative tool in materials science, enabling data-driven discovery, property prediction, and design of new materials. Traditional methods like experimental testing and finite element simulations are often expensive and time-consuming. ML techniques, on the other hand, offer efficient alternatives through predictive modeling and data-driven insights, particularly in areas such as creep behavior analysis, microstructure-property correlation, and optimization of processing parameters. Liang et al. successfully applied artificial neural networks to predict long-term creep life in 9Cr–1Mo–V–Nb steel, demonstrating that ML models can achieve high accuracy even with limited input data \cite{liang2020ann}. Similarly, abductive networks were applied by Wang et al. to forecast rupture life in ferritic steels, showing potential in non-linear pattern extraction \cite{wang2013abductive}.


\subsection{Dimensionality Reduction and Principal Component Analysis (PCA)}

Materials datasets often include numerous features derived from composition, microstructure, or simulation parameters. To handle this complexity, dimensionality reduction techniques such as Principal Component Analysis (PCA) are frequently used. PCA identifies the principal axes of variation in the dataset and projects high-dimensional data onto a lower-dimensional subspace while retaining maximum variance \cite{bishop2006pattern}.

In the context of creep behavior prediction, PCA can reduce the dimensionality of microstructural or stress-strain time-series data, allowing machine learning models to focus on the most informative features. This not only improves computational efficiency but can also reveal underlying physical trends.

\subsection{Surrogate Modeling}

Surrogate models, also known as metamodels or response surface models, are simplified predictive models that approximate the behavior of complex physical systems or simulations. In materials science, surrogate models are widely applied to approximate finite element simulations of creep and deformation, allowing for faster optimization and uncertainty quantification.

Common surrogate modeling techniques include:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Polynomial Regression and Kriging}: Used to interpolate simulation data across parameter spaces.
	\item \textbf{Gaussian Process Regression (GPR)}: Offers uncertainty estimation along with predictions, making it suitable for active learning applications.
	\item \textbf{Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs)}: Capture nonlinear relationships between material features and properties, effective in high-dimensional datasets.
\end{itemize}

These models are crucial in active learning frameworks, where new data points are selected iteratively to improve model performance with minimal simulation or experimental effort \cite{settles2010active}.

\subsection{Integration with Simulation}

Numerical simulations — particularly finite element methods (FEM) — remain central to creep behavior modeling. However, they are computationally intensive. Recent literature shows increasing integration of ML models as surrogate solvers within FEM pipelines. These hybrid approaches accelerate exploration of large design spaces, and have been successfully used to model multi-stage creep curves and time-dependent deformation \cite{wessel2004modelling, kruzic2003limitations}.

Additionally, ML can aid inverse modeling: inferring material parameters or damage laws from experimental creep curves, where traditional optimization is infeasible or underdetermined.

\section{Active Learning and Data-Driven Design}

The growing use of machine learning in materials science has enabled researchers to move from traditional trial-and-error approaches toward data-driven material design and discovery. However, generating high-quality data — whether through experiments or simulations — remains resource-intensive. \textit{Active learning} is an efficient framework designed to mitigate this challenge by intelligently selecting the most informative data points for model training. Further, physics-constrained learning models have emerged as an attractive approach to enhance surrogate accuracy while embedding domain-specific constraints \cite{zhu2019physics, karniadakis2021pinns}. Such methods can be integrated with active learning loops for improved generalization in high-dimensional simulation settings.


\subsection{Motivation for Active Learning in Creep Prediction}

Creep behavior simulations involve evaluating material responses under varying stress, temperature, and microstructural parameters. The parameter space is typically large and high-dimensional, making uniform sampling (e.g., full-factorial or random) computationally inefficient. Additionally, regions of the parameter space may lead to invalid or non-convergent results in numerical models.

Active learning addresses this by enabling:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Efficient exploration} of the design space.
	\item \textbf{Reduction in simulation cost} by focusing only on informative or uncertain regions.
	\item \textbf{Improved generalization} of ML models using fewer training points.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Active Learning Strategies}

Several strategies have been explored in the literature:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Uncertainty Sampling:} Points where the current model is most uncertain are prioritized for evaluation.
	\item \textbf{Query-by-Committee (QBC):} An ensemble of models is trained, and points with the highest disagreement among them are selected \cite{morand2022efficient}.
	\item \textbf{Feasibility-Based Selection:} Particularly useful in simulations where many parameter sets lead to non-physical or non-convergent outputs. A classifier can first be trained to identify valid regions \cite{heese2019optimized}.
\end{itemize}

These techniques help in focusing computational effort where it is most impactful — a major advantage in creep modeling, where simulations are often expensive and time-consuming.

\subsection{Applications in Microstructure–Property Exploration}

In material design tasks, active learning has been used to explore microstructure-property maps, where each microstructure corresponds to a specific mechanical response. For example, in the work by Morand et al. \cite{morand2022efficient}, active learning was used to efficiently explore a 2D microstructure space while avoiding regions leading to physically irrelevant predictions.

Such frameworks are ideal for use in surrogate modeling of creep, where expensive FEM-based simulations can be replaced by efficient regression models trained using active learning loops. The surrogate model can then be used for inverse design — identifying material parameters that produce desired creep curves.

\subsection{Sampling Strategies in Practice}

In this thesis, several sampling strategies are explored to efficiently generate data for surrogate modeling of creep behavior:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Random Sampling:} A basic but often suboptimal strategy, leading to non-uniform coverage of the parameter space.
	
	\item \textbf{Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS):} Ensures stratified sampling across each parameter dimension. However, LHS may still produce uneven distributions in response space, particularly when certain parameters dominate the behavior.
	
	\item \textbf{Logarithmic Sampling:} Used to better capture sensitivity in highly nonlinear regions of the parameter space. For example, when material parameters like the hardening coefficient $b$ span several orders of magnitude, logarithmic sampling ensures sufficient resolution in low-value regions that may otherwise be underrepresented.
	
	\item \textbf{Active Learning:} An iterative strategy where the ML model itself identifies which regions of the design space require more data. Methods include uncertainty sampling and query-by-committee (QBC).
\end{itemize}

Logarithmic sampling is particularly beneficial when exploring creep laws with exponential or power-law behavior, as it avoids oversampling in regions with diminishing returns. By combining LHS with logarithmic scaling and active learning, this thesis aims to balance space-filling coverage with model-aware refinement of the dataset.

Ultimately, these strategies enable a tight feedback loop between simulation and surrogate model improvement, making them ideal for high-dimensional, computationally expensive systems like creep prediction.
