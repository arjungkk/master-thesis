\section{Creep Behavior in Metallic Materials}
\label{Section::Creep_Behavior_in_Metallic_Materials}

\subsection{Fundamentals of Creep}
\label{Section::Fundamentals_of_Creep}

Creep is a time-dependent, permanent deformation that occurs in materials subjected to constant stress below the yield point and elevated temperatures, typically above 30\% of their melting temperature~\cite{frost1982deformation}. In metals, creep can significantly influence the mechanical integrity of components operating in high-temperature environments such as gas turbines, nuclear reactors, and jet engines.

The creep strain, $\epsilon(t, T)$, in a material, is a function of both time$(t)$ and temperature$(T)$ and can be expressed empirically through various models like Norton–Bailey Law, Monkman–Grant Relationship, etc. The rate at which creep deformation occurs is influenced by several factors, including applied stress, grain size, temperature, and the presence of second-phase particles or precipitates~\cite{frost1982deformation, dieter1986mechanical}. 

\subsection{Mechanisms of Creep Deformation}

Creep deformation in metals is governed by several atomic and dislocation-level mechanisms. The dominant mechanism depends on load specific parameters like temperature and stress, and material specific parameters like grain size and diffusion conditions.

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figures/creep_mechanisms.png}
	\caption{Overview of different creep mechanisms based on applied stress and temperature. Reproduced from~\cite{habraken2024lect}.}
	\label{fig:creep_mechanisms}
\end{figure}

The different mechanisms of creep deformation are:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Dislocation Creep:}  
	Dominant at intermediate temperatures and high stresses. It involves the motion of dislocations through the crystal lattice, and the creep rate follows a stress dependent power-law relationship. The creep strain rate is given by the \textbf{Power-Law/Norton's Law}:
	
	\begin{equation}
		\dot{\varepsilon} = A \sigma^n \exp\left(-\frac{Q}{RT}\right)
		\label{eq:powerlaw}
	\end{equation}
	
	where $n$ is the stress exponent (typically 3–8 for metallic materials), $Q$ is activation energy, $R$ is the gas constant, and $T$ is absolute temperature~\cite{frost1982deformation}.
	
	\item \textbf{Diffusion Creep:}
	Includes two mechanisms:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textit{Nabarro–Herring creep:} Occurs at high temperatures and low stresses in materials with large grain sizes. Atoms diffuse through the lattice, causing elongation in the direction of the applied stress and contraction perpendicular to it~\cite{nabarro1948deformation,herring1950diffusional}.
		\item \textit{Coble creep:} Similar to Nabarro-Herring creep but involves diffusion along grain boundaries instead of through the bulk. It is significant in fine-grained materials and becomes prominent at lower temperatures than Nabarro-Herring creep~\cite{coble1963model}.
	\end{itemize}
	
	\item \textbf{Grain Boundary Sliding (GBS):}  
	Grain boundary sliding involves the relative movement of grains along their interfaces and is often accommodated by diffusion or dislocation activity. While not typically a dominant creep mechanism on its own, GBS becomes significant at high homologous temperatures and low to moderate stress levels, particularly in fine-grained materials. In the creep mechanism map (Figure~\ref{fig:creep_mechanisms}), GBS primarily supports diffusion creep by helping the material deform without creating voids at grain boundaries. It can also become prominent during superplastic deformation and cyclic thermal loading, where grain boundary mobility is enhanced~\cite{raj1971grain}.
	
	\item \textbf{Climb-Glide Mechanism:}  
	This mechanism governs deformation in the dislocation creep regime of the creep map, which occurs at moderate to high stress and intermediate to high temperatures. Dislocations move by glide within specific crystallographic planes, but when they encounter obstacles, they must climb by absorbing or emitting vacancies to continue movement. This combined motion allows for sustained plastic deformation even when slip is geometrically restricted. Climb, enabled by atomic diffusion, is often the rate-controlling step in high-temperature dislocation creep~\cite{weertman1957theory}.
	
	\item \textbf{Dynamic Recrystallization (DRX):}  
	Dynamic recrystallization occurs primarily at high temperatures and high strain rates, where dislocation density becomes high enough to trigger the formation of new, strain-free grains during deformation. This process is typically observed beyond the dislocation creep regime, in the plastic domain of the creep mechanism map. DRX acts as a softening mechanism, counteracting strain hardening and enabling continued deformation without failure. It is particularly relevant in hot working of metals and in creep environments approaching the melting point~\cite{gourdet2003model}.
	
	
\end{itemize}

These mechanisms may act simultaneously, with the dominant mechanism shifting depending on the material-specific factors and operating conditions such as stress, temperature, and grain size. While mechanism maps classify deformation regimes based on macroscopic parameters, the underlying microstructural evolution, such as precipitate coarsening, dislocation density changes, and sub-grain formation, is not directly represented. Nevertheless, these microstructural processes strongly influence which creep mechanism becomes active and how it evolves over time. For example, precipitate coarsening can reduce obstacle strength for dislocation motion, while sub-grain formation and dynamic recovery processes can delay the onset of tertiary creep~\cite{chatterjee_creep}.

\section{Numerical Modelling of Creep}
\label{Section::Modelling_Creep}

\subsection{Creep Models}
\label{Section::Creep_Models_Phenomenological}

Creep behaviour has traditionally been modelled using a variety of empirical and semi-physical formulations. These models aim to describe the relationship between stress, temperature, and time-dependent strain, providing analytical tools for creep life prediction. Such models are collectively referred to as \textit{Constitutive models}. Constitutive models relate stress, temperature, and time to strain rate or accumulated strain, and are central to both analytical and numerical simulation frameworks.

The commonly used models/approaches are:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Norton’s Law:} 
	Equation~$~\ref{eq:powerlaw}$ models steady-state creep with a power-law dependency on applied stress. It is the most widely used empirical creep model for engineering design under high temperatures~\cite{frost1982deformation}.
	
	\item \textbf{Larson–Miller Parameter (LMP):} 
	A time–temperature parameter used to predict long-term creep life from short-term test data. It is defined as:
	\[
	P = T(C + \log t_r)
	\]
	where \( P \) is the Larson–Miller parameter, \( T \) is the absolute temperature, \( t_r \) is the time to rupture, and \( C \) is a material-dependent constant. The LMP enables extrapolation of accelerated high-temperature test results to real-world service conditions~\cite{larson1954time}.
	
	\item \textbf{Monkman–Grant Relationship:}  
	An empirical correlation linking the minimum creep rate to rupture life, expressed as:
	\[
	\dot{\varepsilon}_{\text{min}} \cdot t_r = \text{constant}
	\]
	where \( \dot{\varepsilon}_{\text{min}} \) is the minimum (steady-state) creep rate and \( t_r \) is the time to rupture. This relationship is useful for early-stage alloy qualification where long-term data are not yet available~\cite{monkman1956relationship}.
	
	\item \textbf{Wilshire Model:}  
	An extension of the classical power-law creep model that incorporates the yield stress into the stress term for a more physically motivated representation. The steady-state creep rate is given by:
	\[
	\dot{\varepsilon} = \dot{\varepsilon}_0 \left( \frac{\sigma}{\sigma_y} \right)^n \exp\left(-\frac{Q}{RT}\right)
	\]
	where:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \( \dot{\varepsilon} \) - creep strain rate,
		\item \( \dot{\varepsilon}_0 \) - reference strain rate,
		\item \( \sigma \) - applied stress,
		\item \( \sigma_y \) - yield stress at test temperature,
		\item \( n \) - stress exponent,
		\item \( Q \) - activation energy,
		\item \( R \) - gas constant,
		\item \( T \) - absolute temperature.
	\end{itemize}
	
	An alternative form also estimates time to rupture:
	\[
	\ln t_r = A - B \left( \frac{\sigma}{\sigma_y} \right)
	\]
	where \( t_r \) is rupture time and \( A \), \( B \) are temperature-dependent material constants. The Wilshire model is particularly useful for high-strength materials such as martensitic steels and nickel-based superalloys, where classical Norton-Bailey models fail to capture observed non-linear creep behaviour~\cite{wilshire2007creep}.
	
	\item \textbf{Norton-Bailey Law:} 
	The Norton-Bailey law expresses total creep strain as a function of stress and time, typically in the form \( \varepsilon = A \sigma^n t^m \), where \( A \), \( n \), and \( m \) are material constants.

	\item \textbf{Kachanov-Rabotnov Model:} 
	While the models above describe creep rate and rupture time based on phenomenological fits, they do not explicitly account for damage evolution during the tertiary stage of creep. To address this, damage mechanics-based models such as the Kachanov-Rabotnov model introduce a time-evolving damage variable, $\omega(t)$, representing the material’s degradation state~\cite{kachanov1958rupture, rabotnov1969creep}. The creep strain rate and damage evolution are given by:
		
	\[
	\dot{\varepsilon} = A \sigma^n (1 - \omega)^m, \quad \dot{\omega} = B \sigma^n (1 - \omega)^k
	\]
	
	where $A$, $B$, $n$, $m$, and $k$ are material constants. The variable $\omega$ increases from 0 to 1, with failure predicted when $\omega = 1$. Although originally formulated to model the tertiary stage, this model can simulate all three creep stages by selecting appropriate values of the damage evolution parameters.
	
	Recent work by \textit{Sattar et al.}~\cite{sattar2021damage} applied the this formulation to SS-316 stainless steel, comparing it against the classical Norton-Bailey law.  Using regression-based curve fitting, \textit{Sattar et al.} successfully calibrated the Kachanov-Rabotnov model and demonstrated its superior performance in capturing the entire creep lifecycle, including primary, secondary, and tertiary stages.
	
	\item \textbf{Dislocation-Based Formulation:}  
	Dislocation-based and damage-informed constitutive models offer improved physical accuracy for long-term creep predictions, especially under complex loading and microstructural evolution.
	
	\begin{equation}
		\dot{\varepsilon} = \frac{\rho_m b v_g}{M}
	\end{equation}
	
	where \( \rho_m \) is the mobile dislocation density, \( b \) is the Burgers vector\footnote{The Burgers vector represents the magnitude and direction of lattice distortion caused by a dislocation; it is a fundamental parameter in dislocation theory.}, \( v_g \) is the dislocation glide velocity, and \( M \) is the Taylor factor accounting for polycrystalline effects.
	
	Advanced formulations extend this model by evolving internal state variables, such as:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Static and mobile dislocation densities
		\item Sub-grain size and boundary effects
		\item Cavitation and damage
	\end{itemize}
	
	These models have been developed and applied in finite element simulations by researchers such as \textit{Ghoniem et al.}~\cite{ghoniem2003dislocation} and recent European research initiatives focused on crystal plasticity and creep-fatigue interaction~\cite{morch2022creep, haruman2020continuum}.
	
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Finite Element Analysis}

Finite element analysis (FEA) provides a powerful framework for simulating creep deformation in complex geometries and loading conditions. Constitutive models describing creep behaviour are embedded into material subroutines and solved within FEA codes. LAGAMINE\footnote{LAGAMINE is a finite element code, born at the University of Liege, initially developed for the digital modelling of large deformations, mechanical problems and contact problems. It allows to predict the damages and rupture along the process of formation, as well as the static and specific fatigue load. Simulations can be extended to the modelling of processes such as deep-drawing, straightening, cold rolling, continuous casting, incremental forming, vertical lancer combined, superplastic forming or former microforming. In these simulations, the shaping of metal, of new elements such as solid-shell elements or adapted darning strategies have also been developed within LAGAMINE~\cite{lagamine_uliege}.}, the FEA tool used in this thesis, includes time-integration schemes suitable for large strain, time-dependent plasticity problems, enabling robust modelling of the full creep lifecycle.

Numerical models offer the advantage of extrapolating creep behavior under untested conditions. However, these models require extensive calibration and may become computationally expensive for high-dimensional parametric studies.

\subsection{Limitations of Classical Numerical Methods}

Despite their accuracy, classical numerical models face several limitations:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{High computational cost:} Especially when exploring large material parameter spaces or performing uncertainty quantification.
	\item \textbf{Sensitivity to parameterization:} Requires precise identification of model constants, which may not be available from experiments.
	\item \textbf{Limited extrapolation:} Classical models may fail outside calibrated regimes or when microstructural effects are dominant.
\end{itemize}

As described in~\cite{wessel2004modelling}, these challenges motivate the development of surrogate models that approximate the numerical outputs with significantly reduced computational effort.


\section{Machine Learning in Materials Science}

In recent years, machine learning (ML) has emerged as a transformative tool in materials science, enabling data-driven discovery, property prediction, and design of new materials. Traditional methods like experimental testing and finite element simulations are often expensive and time-consuming. ML techniques, on the other hand, offer efficient alternatives through predictive modelling and data-driven insights, particularly in areas such as creep behavior analysis, microstructure-property correlation, and optimization of processing parameters. Liang et al. successfully applied artificial neural networks to predict long-term creep life in 9Cr–1Mo–V–Nb steel, demonstrating that ML models can achieve high accuracy even with limited input data~\cite{liang2020ann}. Similarly, abductive networks were applied by Wang et al. to forecast rupture life in ferritic steels, showing potential in non-linear pattern extraction~\cite{wang2013abductive}.


\subsection{Dimensionality Reduction and Principal Component Analysis (PCA)}

Materials datasets often include numerous features derived from composition, microstructure, or simulation parameters. To handle this complexity, dimensionality reduction techniques such as Principal Component Analysis (PCA) are frequently used. PCA identifies the principal axes of variation in the dataset and projects high-dimensional data onto a lower-dimensional subspace while retaining maximum variance~\cite{bishop2006pattern}.

In the context of creep behavior prediction, PCA can reduce the dimensionality of microstructural or stress-strain time-series data, allowing machine learning models to focus on the most informative features. This not only improves computational efficiency but can also reveal underlying physical trends.

\subsection{Surrogate modelling}

Surrogate models, also known as metamodels or response surface models, are simplified predictive models that approximate the behavior of complex physical systems or simulations. In materials science, surrogate models can be applied to approximate finite element simulations of creep and deformation, allowing for faster optimization and uncertainty quantification.

Common surrogate modelling techniques include:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Polynomial Regression and Kriging}: Used to interpolate simulation data across parameter spaces.
	\item \textbf{Gaussian Process Regression (GPR)}: Offers uncertainty estimation along with predictions, making it suitable for active learning applications.
	\item \textbf{Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs)}: Capture nonlinear relationships between material features and properties, effective in high-dimensional datasets.
\end{itemize}

These models are crucial in active learning frameworks, where new data points are selected iteratively to improve model performance with minimal simulation or experimental effort~\cite{settles2010active}.

\subsection{Integration with Simulation}

Numerical simulations — particularly finite element methods (FEM) — remain central to creep behavior modelling. However, they are computationally intensive. Recent literature shows increasing integration of ML models as surrogate solvers within FEM pipelines. These hybrid approaches accelerate exploration of large design spaces, and have been successfully used to model multi-stage creep curves and time-dependent deformation~\cite{wessel2004modelling, kruzic2003limitations}.

Additionally, ML can aid inverse modelling: inferring material parameters or damage laws from experimental creep curves, where traditional optimization is infeasible or underdetermined.

\section{Active Learning and Data-Driven Design}

The growing use of machine learning in materials science has enabled researchers to move from traditional trial-and-error approaches toward data-driven material design and discovery. However, generating high-quality data — whether through experiments or simulations — remains resource-intensive. \textit{Active learning} is an efficient framework designed to mitigate this challenge by intelligently selecting the most informative data points for model training. Further, physics-constrained learning models have emerged as an attractive approach to enhance surrogate accuracy while embedding domain-specific constraints~\cite{zhu2019physics, karniadakis2021pinns}. Such methods can be integrated with active learning loops for improved generalization in high-dimensional simulation settings.


\subsection{Motivation for Active Learning in Creep Prediction}

Creep behavior simulations involve evaluating material responses under varying stress, temperature, and microstructural parameters. The parameter space is typically large and high-dimensional, making uniform sampling (e.g., full-factorial or random) computationally inefficient. Additionally, regions of the parameter space may lead to invalid or non-convergent results in numerical models.

Active learning addresses this by enabling:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Efficient exploration} of the design space.
	\item \textbf{Reduction in simulation cost} by focusing only on informative or uncertain regions.
	\item \textbf{Improved generalization} of ML models using fewer training points.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Active Learning Strategies}

Several strategies have been explored in the literature:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Uncertainty Sampling:} Points where the current model is most uncertain are prioritized for evaluation.
	\item \textbf{Query-by-Committee (QBC):} An ensemble of models is trained, and points with the highest disagreement among them are selected~\cite{morand2022efficient}.
	\item \textbf{Feasibility-Based Selection:} Particularly useful in simulations where many parameter sets lead to non-physical or non-convergent outputs. A classifier can first be trained to identify valid regions~\cite{heese2019optimized}.
\end{itemize}

These techniques help in focusing computational effort where it is most impactful — a major advantage in creep modelling, where simulations are often expensive and time-consuming.

\subsection{Applications in Microstructure–Property Exploration}

In material design tasks, active learning has been used to explore microstructure-property maps, where each microstructure corresponds to a specific mechanical response. For example, in the work by Morand et al.~\cite{morand2022efficient}, active learning was used to efficiently explore a 2D microstructure space while avoiding regions leading to physically irrelevant predictions.

Such frameworks are ideal for use in surrogate modelling of creep, where expensive FEM-based simulations can be replaced by efficient regression models trained using active learning loops. The surrogate model can then be used for inverse design — identifying material parameters that produce desired creep curves.

\subsection{Sampling Strategies in Practice}

In this thesis, several sampling strategies are explored to efficiently generate data for surrogate modelling of creep behavior:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Random Sampling:} A basic but often suboptimal strategy, leading to non-uniform coverage of the parameter space.
	
	\item \textbf{Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS):} Ensures stratified sampling across each parameter dimension. However, LHS may still produce uneven distributions in response space, particularly when certain parameters dominate the behavior.
	
	\item \textbf{Logarithmic Sampling:} Used to better capture sensitivity in highly nonlinear regions of the parameter space. For example, when material parameters like the hardening coefficient $b$ span several orders of magnitude, logarithmic sampling ensures sufficient resolution in low-value regions that may otherwise be underrepresented.
	
	\item \textbf{Active Learning:} An iterative strategy where the ML model itself identifies which regions of the design space require more data. Methods include uncertainty sampling and query-by-committee (QBC).
\end{itemize}

Logarithmic sampling is particularly beneficial when exploring creep laws with exponential or power-law behavior, as it avoids oversampling in regions with diminishing returns. By combining LHS with logarithmic scaling and active learning, this thesis aims to balance space-filling coverage with model-aware refinement of the dataset.

Ultimately, these strategies enable a tight feedback loop between simulation and surrogate model improvement, making them ideal for high-dimensional, computationally expensive systems like creep prediction.
